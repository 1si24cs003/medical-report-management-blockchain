## 1. Introduction

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) constitute the critical operational foundation of modern digital healthcare, consolidating diagnostic images, laboratory blood panels, physician clinical notes, surgical records, and prescription bills. However, prevailing healthcare architectures continue to rely upon centralized database structures or siloed hospital management systems. Centralized repositories suffer from significant vulnerabilities:
1. **Single Points of Failure & Ransomware Vulnerability:** A single compromised server or credential can expose hundreds of thousands of patient records or halt hospital operations.
2. **Data Tamper Susceptibility:** Centralized records lack mathematical immutability; privileged administrative accounts or unauthorized insiders can alter diagnostic metrics without creating permanent, non-repudiable audit logs.
3. **Repeated Diagnostic Testing:** Fragmentation across institutions leaves prior investigations inaccessible during emergency or referral consultations, forcing patients to undergo costly, repetitive blood tests and radiological scans.
4. **Data Redundancy:** Conventional repositories do not verify cryptographic file uniqueness prior to storage, leading to bloated servers storing duplicate report files.

To resolve these challenges, extensive academic and industrial research has investigated Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) and cryptographic hashing. The following literature survey evaluates fifteen seminal papers published between 2024 and 2025, analyzing their contributions, architectures, and inherent limitations.

## 3. Critical Synthesis of Identified Limitations (Sections 3.1 – 3.13)

By evaluating the 15 surveyed papers, thirteen systematic architectural limitations were identified:

1. **High Cryptographic Complexity (3.1):** Existing proposals mandate complex proxy re-encryption (PRE) and multi-authority attribute-based encryption (ABE). While mathematically sound, these schemes are nearly impossible to reliably implement, maintain, and audit in practical hospital clinic environments.
2. **Computational & Storage Bloat (3.2):** Committing multi-megabyte diagnostic reports (MRI scans, high-resolution radiographs) directly into blockchain blocks replicates enormous binary blobs across every consensus node, triggering ledger bloat.
3. **Absence of Complete Privacy on Public Ledgers (3.3):** Immutability is a double-edged sword. Writing personal identifying information (PII) directly to a blockchain violates the "Right to be Forgotten" (GDPR Art. 17) and exposes health data to public scrutiny.
4. **Access Revocation Inflexibility (3.4):** Once a doctor is issued an asymmetric decryption key, revoking that clinician's access after a consultation requires complex re-encryption of all historical patient files.
5. **Consortium Governance Scalability (3.5):** Heavyweight architectures experience steep throughput degradation (TPS bottlenecks) as the number of participating clinics expands.
6. **Healthcare System Interoperability Gaps (3.6):** Standalone research frameworks fail to harmonize disparate proprietary electronic medical record formats.
7. **Infrastructure Overhead (3.7):** Enterprise networks like Hyperledger Fabric require dedicated ordering clusters, Certificate Authorities, and complex container fleets unviable for standalone regional clinics.
8. **Inability to Handle Large Medical Binary Files (3.8):** Direct on-chain storage fails for diagnostic imaging datasets.
9. **Lack of Production-Grade Implementations (3.9):** Most published papers remain conceptual models or simulated mathematical equations lacking complete, operable source code.
10. **Regulatory Compliance Non-Alignment (3.10):** Strict healthcare mandates (HIPAA, GDPR, HITECH) require patient consent management and data minimization, which naive blockchain ledgers violate.
11. **Overly Narrow Domain Focus (3.11):** Existing papers focus strictly on one isolated sub-problem (e.g. IoT telemetry only or PKI certificate management only).
12. **Inflexible Access Control Schemes (3.12):** Fine-grained ABE frameworks introduce massive latency during acute care triage.
13. **Absence of an Integrated Medical-Report Distribution Model (3.13):** There is an acute lack of a practical, end-to-end framework integrating file upload, duplicate prevention, chronological timeline retrieval, time-expiring temporary access, and instant anti-forgery verification.

## 4. How the Proposed System Addresses These Limitations

Our project, **Medical Report Management & Distribution System on Blockchain**, directly resolves these thirteen bottlenecks through five architectural pillars:
* **Separation of Storage & Proof:** Diagnostic files are encrypted off-chain using **AES-256-CBC**, while the permissioned blockchain ledger stores exclusively pseudonymous Report IDs, SHA-256 hashes, timestamps, and storage pointers.
* **Objective 2 Cryptographic Duplicate Detection:** Before committing an upload, the backend computes the SHA-256 hash. If an identical hash is already present on the blockchain, the upload is rejected (HTTP 409), preventing redundant storage and guaranteeing single-source veracity.
* **Feature 7.1 Anti-Forgery QR Verification:** Doctors verify any diagnostic report instantly by scanning its embedded QR code, triggering a real-time SHA-256 recalculation against the immutable blockchain anchor (yielding MATCH vs MISMATCH).
* **Feature 7.2 Time-Expiring Secure Access Links:** Patients generate cryptographically signed JWT consultation links with custom expiration windows (e.g., 2 minutes for demo, 1 hour for consultation). When the token expires, access is automatically revoked without complex key re-issuance.
* **Feature 7.3 Smart Keyword Extraction (OCR):** Tesseract.js converts report imagery into machine-readable text and indexes clinical keywords ("Blood Sugar", "Fracture", "Hemoglobin") in database tags, enabling fast search without requiring off-chain file decryption.
